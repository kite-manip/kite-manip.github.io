Abstract

Generalizing manipulation policies across robot embodiments remains difficult because standard policies tie the interactions needed for a task to the actions of a particular robot. We study zero-shot cross-embodiment manipulation, where a policy trained on source embodiments must be deployed on a structurally different target embodiment without additional task demonstrations. We introduce Kinematic Interaction Transfer across Embodiments (KITE), which decouples manipulation into embodiment-agnostic interaction prediction and embodiment-specific kinematic decoding, connected through a learned representation of interaction intent based on contact patterns. An interaction policy is trained to predict the intent using demonstration data only collected on a source robot, and shared across morphologically different target robots. A kinematic decoder realizes these intents by learning from synthetic data generated from each embodiment's kinematic model. Through such decoupling, KITE can robustly transfer learned skills to a new embodiment given only its kinematic model, without recollecting any task-specific demonstration data. We evaluate KITE on four manipulation tasks in simulation and the real world spanning transfer between parallel grippers, dexterous hands, and composite embodiments. KITE successfully achieves zero-shot transfer to structurally different target embodiments, and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines.

Kinematic Interaction Transfer across Embodiments (KITE)

KITE enables cross-embodiment transfer without recollecting demonstration data for target robots. It decouples manipulation into embodiment-agnostic interaction prediction and embodiment-specific kinematic decoding, connected through a learned, contact-based representation of interaction intent.

At deployment, the interaction policy predicts the next latent intent from live observations, and the target's kinematic decoder converts it into a motor command. For an embodiment outside the pretrained set, we add its input and output projectors and fine-tune the decoder bank using synthetic kinematic data from the existing and new embodiments. The intent encoder stays fixed, so existing interaction policies are reused unchanged.

Simulated Experiments

We evaluate four tasks across six robot embodiments: Robotiq, Barrett, Allegro, Wuji, XHand, and Robotiq+Stick. The intent encoder and decoder bank are pretrained on the kinematic models of all involved embodiments, then frozen and reused across tasks. Each task has its own interaction policy, trained only on source demonstrations.

KITE achieves an average success rate of 86.4% across 12 task–target settings. Each target is evaluated on 100 held-out episodes per seed, with results averaged across three random seeds.

Objects Placing

Robotiq demonstrations transfer to Barrett, Allegro, and Wuji. The robot picks up an object with randomized position and orientation and places it inside a nearby bin. Training and testing use the same six objects.

Keyboard Pressing

Wuji demonstrations transfer pressing three target keys to Barrett, Allegro, and Robotiq+Stick. Success requires depressing every target key past 50% of its travel, testing spatial precision.

Bottles Pumping

Wuji demonstrations transfer to Barrett, Allegro, and Robotiq+Stick across three pump bottles. Success requires lifting the bottle and depressing its pump by at least 1 cm each. Robotiq+Stick distributes supporting and pressing contacts across a gripper and a separately actuated stick.

Nut Rotating

XHand demonstrations transfer sustained contact coordination to Barrett, Allegro, and Wuji. The nut rotates about a fixed stud, and all hand bases remain fixed. Each episode is scored by the target's net rotation divided by the source demonstration's net rotation, clipped to [0, 1].

Real-World Experiments

We evaluate all four tasks on a Wuji hand mounted on a Tianji Marvin arm, using the same frozen kinematic decoder without target-task demonstrations or real-world fine-tuning. Objects placing transfers from Robotiq, nut rotating from XHand, and keyboard pressing and bottles pumping from human demonstrations.

At deployment, a calibrated ZED camera provides object point clouds filtered with SAM 2 segmentation masks. Each task is evaluated over 10 trials.

Objects Placing

Robotiq → Wuji succeeds in 8/10 trials, picking up an object and placing it inside a nearby bin.

Keyboard Pressing

Human hand → Wuji succeeds in 8/10 trials. Success requires depressing all three target keys past 50% of their travel.

Bottles Pumping

Human hand → Wuji succeeds in 6/10 trials, lifting the bottle and depressing its pump by at least 1 cm each.

Nut Rotating

XHand → Wuji succeeds in 7/10 trials. Success requires rotating the nut by more than π radians.

Analysis of KITE

We examine how KITE extends to a new embodiment and how the latent intent interface and decoder supervision affect transfer. We also test sensitivity to the target's initial pose to understand the limits of kinematic decoding.

Predicting raw contact slots instead of latent intents reduces average success by over 30 percentage points on each of the first three tasks. The largest individual drop is on Wuji nut rotating, from 89.3% to 27.3%. The learned interface maps unordered, variable-size contact sets to a fixed-dimensional action space.

Replaying the same source-intent sequences from different initial hand poses preserves success under modest perturbations, but performance drops with large shifts or a flipped hand. This is consistent with the decoder's local training construction, which does not guarantee intent realization from arbitrary initializations.

Execution Diversity

During objects placing, the Wuji decoder uses different finger combinations across objects, although all demonstrations come from the same Robotiq gripper. It realizes the interaction intents without a prescribed source-to-target part mapping.

Comparative Results

We compare KITE (Oracle Intent) with SPIDER to isolate kinematic decoding from interaction prediction. Both use source trajectories: Oracle Intent supplies the frozen decoder with intents encoded from source demonstrations, while SPIDER optimizes target motion using manually specified part correspondences. On bottles pumping, SPIDER loses contact while the kinematic decoder preserves the supporting and pressing interactions.
